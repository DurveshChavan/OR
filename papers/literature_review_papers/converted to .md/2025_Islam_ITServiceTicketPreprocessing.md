# **_Automated Data Classification And Pre-Processing Of It Service Tickets For Future Machine Learning Predictions_** 

Faraz Islam<sup>1</sup> , Tasneem Ahmed*<sup>2</sup> 

1,2 Department of Computer Application, Integral University, Lucknow, India-226026, *Corresponding Author: Tasneem Ahmed* 

**_Abstract:_** Nowadays, many IT help desks depend on ticket systems when dealing with issues, inquiries, or system failures. Working through these manually often drags the pace, consumes extra work hours, and puts schedules under pressure. Although operational platforms gather vast amounts of textual records, those rarely fit algorithmic needs without adjustment. A lot comes cluttered - filled with irregularities that block smooth analysis. Consider missing pieces of information, duplicated entries, inconsistent formats, embedded snippets of code, misplaced tags, multilingual phrases, or extended freeform descriptions tough to interpret by machines. One way to study raw IT support tickets is by tracking their path across an entire system, beginning at ManageEngine ServiceDesk Plus, where they are made. From there, they travel toward processing inside a private SQL Server environment. Step follows step using R-based utilities that tidy and reshape textual data ahead of further analysis. Early on, detection shifts focus to non-English texts, and these get set aside quickly. Following that stage, duplicates are removed, leaving behind only distinct words ready for a closer look. Once filtered, words without clear value disappear. Then comes reducing terms to basic versions, so “running” becomes “run”, evening out small mismatches. Strange marks or uneven layout get removed. Every line shift into a consistent format, and later on, the key terms become clearer once the focus shifts towards the most repeated words. Visual maps of frequent word pairings uncover patterns not obvious at first glance. Meanwhile, broader examinations help keep rare outliers from skewing interpretation. From a total of 33,059 service tickets, every data entry contains structured fields alongside text columns such as Subject and Description. The results part shows that rigorous data cleansing improves the data consistency, produces a high-quality dataset, and reduces noise, which is suitable for training datasets using a machine learning algorithm. For future work, the focus will be on integrating the cleaned dataset with Azure Machine Learning Studio for automated ticket dispatching. Also, aiming at the optimization of the proposed model through hyperparameter tuning, continuous retraining with live datasets, and real-time deployment to enhance the service efficiency, SLA compliance, and overall satisfaction of the users. 

**_Keywords:_** IT Service Management (ITSM), Ticket Classification, Text Pre- processing, Data Cleansing, ManageEngine ServiceDesk Plus (MSP). 

### **I.** **_Introduction_** 

In the modern digital ecosystem, companies accumulate large amounts of unstructured text, especially on the IT support websites of the company [1, 2]. The records left by users (help tickets, summaries of an issue, reports of an event, and messages between each other) bring together large and extensive data sets; when carefully handled, these data sets provide valuable insights into patterns and avenues of automated solutions. However, as the information is not touched or corrupted, it becomes useless. Before analyzing or applying machine-learning methods, data cleansing becomes critically important, not optional, beforehand. The raw data generated by instruments implemented in controlled services are often full of errors: spelling mistakes coexist with malfunctioning designs, duplicate records appear, unnecessary tags remain, and capitalization is unpredictable and unsteady in nature [3]. Normally ignored, ordinary words like are, the or her are common in what users provide, and so are punctuations and numbers, which can hardly be used in the analysis to provide semantics to the text at all [4]. Left unchecked, these unprocessed inputs have a possibility of biasing results in activities like clustering, labeling, or intent detection [5]. This paper is based on the

lifecycle of textual data in total, such as its extraction, refinement, and preparation to be used in training models to be automated. With examples of real records of IT helpdesk, this work outlines over ten crucial cleaning operations. They are deleting redundant entries, deleting unused fields, filtering the repetitive or uncommon words, converting verbs to their stem forms, deleting embedded code markers, removing the non-letter characters, and standardizing the cases of all characters [6]. One of the central reasons why machine learning fails in non-controlled laboratory conditions is associated with poor text preparation [7]. Even though the lion's share of research is often given to algorithmic development, the quality of raw data is often given second-best consideration. In the context of natural language processing, such an imbalance is often vividly expressed by the situation in which valuable information is often lost in the noise of the background noise [5]. Lack of stringent cleaning protocols leads to poor pattern identification, causing distorted outputs and poor handling of new cases. 

Fundamentally, this study highlights the importance of accurate data refinement as the underlying platform to automatically route IT tickets. Processed textual information makes it easier to perform better tokenization, count frequencies, and separate representations of features to use in training models [8]. It has been shown that the deletion of digits attached to letters, the unnecessary whitespace, and the replacement of Latin-1 symbols with simple ASCII characters can make the entries more consistent. Omission of non-standard stop phrases, e.g., names of staff, names of systems or teams, etc., eliminates factors that do not give any analytical value. Having removed such distractions, algorithms may be used to pinpoint meaningful content in contrast to focusing on the insignificant repetitions [4]. The visual analyses that are used through word clouds help in discovering any rare or dominant terms; elimination of the word clouds is known to sharpen the corpus and make it express more helpful and dense signals. The resulting dataset is therefore narrower with signal predominating noise. Moreover, the stemming procedures and case normalization decrease the number of distinct tokens, thus easing the process of pattern finding. In the process of stemming, morphological variants, like running, are reduced to a canonical form, such as run, which allows word variants to be more readily handled in a consistent way [9]. Lowercasing all text makes the cases of different instances of the same word similar and thus eliminates duplication during feature construction. One particular problem investigated in this paper concerns the elimination of latent XML and HTML tags that are common in ITSM export, which add visual noise to the analysis and block it until it is eliminated [6]. 

Treatment of irregular text structures represents the other aspect of this work, which involves content coded in Latin-1 characters or phrases joined together by omitted spaces. By fixing such anomalies, tokenization and parsing can be performed effectively, which are necessary conditions before further NLP processes can be performed. An important cleaning process involves the elimination of quotations, unnecessary whitespace, and sorting out of numbers that have no meaning to the text [7]. These features are minor in scale but can disproportionately bias the distribution of word frequencies and change the relationship between terms. Getting rid of alphanumeric bits like error404 helps to narrow down to words that have significance in the dataset. On the whole, this study shows the relevance of the preprocessing process in converting raw ITSM tickets into machine-readable ones [10]. The findings show that the text preprocessing of quality can directly influence the functionality of classification systems when accomplished as a part of automated workflows. It is not an easy list of cleaning operations, as the approach offers general structures that could be further developed in terms of thorough research of the real-time text sorting and smart support ticket management, where such a tool as Azure ML and R-based models could be used in particular.

### **II.** **_Literature Review_** 

The studies are unanimous about the importance of text data cleaning in terms of dealing with NLP and ML systems. Preparation of input is not a small matter; it dictates what models will be able to learn - the results of such works as Ghosh et al. [11] are resolute. Han et al. [12] solidify this conception, proving that a practice of quality of raw text literally directly changes the results in the domains of heavy written traffic, e.g., tech support desks. For decades, thousands of papers have studied the ways of organizing unstructured language inputs. The first step for some is to get rid of common filler words; the second is to restructure the verbs by stem cutting or by mapping out roots. Replacing letters with similar ones frequently occurs together with the erasing of the letters that are no longer required. Manning, together with Schütze [13], as these steps reduce junk and redundant sections, patterns of words are simplified, besides decreasing processing in computers. Recently, the focus has shifted to the issue of the removal of stop words in relation to particular domains. General lists generated by such tools as NLTK or spaCy often have limited application when used with corporate data. Rather, specific collections - collections that screen out things such as employee codes, automated messages, or the re-use of administrative wording- are more likely to increase accuracy in the company setting, as results like those of Parameswaran et al. suggest [14]. In the case of clean-up services related to IT services, it can be seen that when it comes to cleaning service management, the cleaning service request records are seldom penetrated. Nonetheless, such attempts were made by Rahman et al. [15] and Joshi et al. [16] have presented ways of classifying helpdesk tickets with the use of simple natural language processing procedures. Some of these studies are incomplete in terms of exhaustive ways of cleaning data, where usually textual inputs are assumed to be available to be utilized. Due to this assumption, the project pays attention to a complete chain of actions that are required to prepare ITSM tickets and make them useful for the implementation of machine learning applications. Other studies consider visuals such as word clouds when performing preliminary data cleanup. Kumar et al.  [17], before training models, identify the words that are rarely read or are messy, and can be made easier by pointing out these displays. Cutting odd words is quicker since they appear clearly. Although their primary task is exploration, feature sharpening is performed step by step, adjusting to them. Ready-made NLP solutions are encountered frequently in previous practice, but scholars believe that custom-made solutions better suit the business purpose. Custom flows are more important in cases when the real-world systems require accuracy [18, 19, 20]. The papers that have been written about earlier support this requirement based on industry examples. The structured tools, in particular MSP, where the information may include HTML/XML tags, coded symbols, or unusual layouts, are especially applicable [21]. Even though there is little previous research regarding the means of dealing with these quirks, this study addresses them directly. 

In retrospect, advances in deep learning and transformers did not reduce the necessity of well-structured and neat data. Even dynamic models such as BERT and GPT cannot work without sound input to operate. By passing by raw information without first going through steam cleaning techniques, models can cling onto noise, making a distorted rendition of broken phrases and then providing poor results [22]. There is one interesting result - preprocessing influences results more than it would. Recent work by Kumar et al. [23] demonstrates the reduction of power requirement in IoT systems through smart data handling. The technique they use is meticulous refinement, and then they start modeling. Efficaciousness is not only the byproduct of the algorithms, but also predilection in design. The real-world tests showed apparent improvement when adjusted during the feature extraction. Using transformer architectures like BERT and RoBERTa, Ahmad, along with

## **_III.IV.iii. Cleaning_** 

The contents of the textual information in the Subject and Description columns are full of extraneous factors, and only the keywords are necessary. As a result, two new columns (SubjectKeywords and DescripKeywords) with unfiltered keywords were developed. This conversion will make the subsequent work of analysis of texts smoother with the help of machine learning algorithms. The procedures involved in cleaning up the research data before the actual study are shown in Algorithm 3. 

### **_Algorithm 3: Ticket Data Cleaning and Keyword Extraction_** 

**_Require_** : Raw ticket dataset containing structured and textual fields **_Ensure_** _:_ Cleaned dataset with extracted keyword features 

1. Remove duplicate ticket records based on the unique request identifier. 

2. Select relevant attributes, including category, subcategory, description, and timestamp. 

3. Subset the dataset to a fixed number of records for analysis. 

4. Rename textual fields for consistency. 

5. Remove HTML and XML tags from text fields. 

6. Convert Latin1-encoded characters to ASCII format. 

7. Normalize whitespace by removing extra spaces. 

8. Insert spaces between concatenated lowercase and uppercase words. 

9. Remove special characters, underscores, and encoded quotation artifacts. 

10. Clean subject text by removing redundant encoded terms. 

11. Remove domain-specific and multilingual stopwords from subject keywords. 

12. Apply stemming to reduce words to their root form. 

13. Remove non-alphanumeric symbols from description keywords. 

14. Normalize quotation marks and spacing in description text. 

15. Apply stemming to description keywords. 

16. Store the cleaned and transformed dataset for downstream analysis.

## **IV.I.** **_Results and Discussion_** 

Here, the section defines the outcome of preprocessing and exploratory analysis of the information on IT services tickets and explains its conclusion on the classification provided by the machine learning, as well as the automation of the process of managing the services. The preprocessing pipeline was implemented to convert raw, highly noisy data in the service desk to structured, high-quality data that can be used in predictive modelling. The data obtained through ManageEngine ServiceDesk Plus was 33,059 tickets, and they contained both structured and unstructured data, including subject and description fields. The first inspection showed that there are a few data quality problems: duplication of records, disparate text encoding, the use of XML/HTML tags, bilingual data, and incorrect formatting. These were the causes of higher noise and hindered the results of predictive models. Other works in the recent literature have made similar observations about the negative impact of noisy ITSM data on machine-learning classifiers [40, 39]. Language recognition was used to keep only the English-language tickets, which gave a homogeneous corpus. Removal of duplicate tickets was based on the uniqueness of request identifiers, and irrelevant attributes were to be removed to increase computational efficiency. Text cleaning, which involves removing HTML/XML tags, whitespace normalization, and case normalization, concatenation, word separation, and removal of special characters and numbers, made important contributions to the consistency of text. Word removal (including domain-specific words) that was then enhanced by stemming minimized vocabulary and facilitated term generalization. The extraction of keywords in the subject and description fields created small sets of features, which concentrated on the issue-oriented terms of concern [41, 42]. 

The word cloud visualization showed that the dominant words were directly related to the service issues, system errors, and the infrastructure components, and low-frequency and generic words were eliminated. An exploratory analysis of the trends in the tickets revealed the constant rise in requests for service, thus indicating the existing operational difficulties. These findings prove that efficient preprocessing boosts the signal-to-noise ratio and creates a solid base to extract features and classify them effectively [43, 44]. Overall, the results confirm that data quality is a key factor in machine- learning success in IT service management. Normalized text is easier to process, tokenize, give better termfrequency distributions, and explain the division between categories, thus making automated classification and routing of tickets possible and accurate with normalized text -reading systems in place [39, 42]. 

### **V.** **_Conclusion_** 

Thus, this paper presents a comprehensive system of processing pre-IT service ticket information, thus making it easier to classify with machine learning. The unstructured raw data were transformed into high-quality data of high quality, trained to be read by the machine learning, through systematic cleansing and regularization, and keyword extraction. Primary issues, including duplicate records, inconsistency in formatting, XML/HTML tags, multilingual and noisy artifacts, were appropriately resolved. Very important to the performance of machine-learning classifiers is the quality of the training dataset. This way is the best way to achieve proper and reliable classification by ensuring that there is proper preprocessing of data to achieve less miss- routing and achieve optimal functioning efficiency.
