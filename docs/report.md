# AI-Driven SLA Optimization: Predictive Ticket Allocation, Escalation and Hardware Replacement in IT Service Desks

**Project report.** Every number below is produced by code in this repository and
is traceable to a committed artifact. Where a number is an assumption rather than
a measurement, the text says so at the point it is used.

---

## 1. What was built, in one paragraph

An IT service desk receives tickets with a deadline attached. This project
estimates, at the moment a ticket is created, the calibrated probability that it
will breach that deadline; estimates how long it will take to resolve;
recommends which resolver should take it, given who holds the required skill, who
is free and how fast each is on that skill; records that recommendation as an
immutable row with a human-readable reason; lets a dispatcher accept or override
it with a stated reason; and keeps every step in an append-only history the
database itself refuses to edit. The dispatch policy was then evaluated against
three disclosed baselines by replaying a public ServiceNow event log under
declared capacity assumptions.

It also keeps a machine register, and advises on replacement from it: the
deteriorating-item average-cost method decides when a machine should be replaced,
and a group-versus-individual comparison decides whether a fleet is better
swapped on a schedule.

The application is `api/servicedesk/` and `client/src/app/`. The evaluation is
`api/servicedesk/eval/`. The Operations Research methods are `api/engine/`, and
they are a section of the product — `/methods` — rather than a separate demo:
the assignment method chooses a resolver on the queue screen and the replacement
method is what the hardware screen computes.

## 2. The contribution claim, and what it deliberately is not

> A leakage-controlled, reproducible evaluation of an auditable SLA-risk-aware
> dispatch policy against disclosed routing baselines on a public ServiceNow
> event log, reported with its null results.

The last four words were added after the replay ran, and section 7 explains why.

The claim is an **implementation and evaluation** claim. It is not an
algorithmic novelty claim. A literature search recorded with its queries and
its date in `docs/literature_gap_audit.md` found that prior work already
predicts SLA outcomes and completion times on this exact log, so neither using
UCI-498 nor predicting `made_sla` on it is new, and this report does not say
otherwise.

What the search did not find is any work that replays this log as a *dispatch*
problem — an exogenous job list, a comparison against FIFO, priority-only and
duration-first, and a per-decision record a reviewer could audit afterwards.
That absence comes from a web-only search on 2026-09-10 with no access to
paywalled bibliographic databases, and it is reported as weak evidence rather
than as a clear field.

Nothing in this project is described as novel, state-of-the-art, patentable, a
guaranteed SLA reduction, a proven causal improvement, or an AI that chooses the
best employee. Those phrases are banned in `docs/literature_gap_audit.md` and
they do not appear here.

## 3. Data

Source: **UCI-498, "Incident management process enriched event log"** — an
anonymised ServiceNow log from one IT company, 141,712 events over 24,918
incidents, March 2016 to February 2017.
`https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/498/incident+management+process+enriched+event+log`

Full provenance, SHA-256 and preparation commands are in
`docs/data_provenance.md`; the audit that produced the decisions below is
`data/reports/uci498_audit.md`, generated by
`python -m api.servicedesk.ingest.uci498 audit`.

Four decisions from that audit constrain everything downstream.

**The breach label is usable, and this was checked rather than assumed.**
`made_sla` latches: across all 24,918 incidents it is `T` throughout, `F`
throughout, or exactly one `T→F` transition. There are zero un-breaches. A naive
reading — "the field changes during 36.6% of incidents, so it is unstable" — is
wrong; that change *is* the breach event. The label is the final ordered
record's value. Prepared set: **23,323 incidents, 38.28% breach prevalence**.

**`closed_at` is not a completion time.** `closed_at − resolved_at` has an
inter-quartile range of about 120.2–120.8 hours: a fixed five-day auto-close
timer, with 25.4% of incidents piling into a single one-hour band. Duration is
therefore `resolved_at − opened_at`. This diverges from the dataset's own
documentation, which names `closed_at` as the dependent variable for
completion-time prediction, and the divergence is deliberate.

**`assignment_group` is leakage and is excluded.** It is populated on 96.4% of
first records — that is a routing action a human already took, which is exactly
the decision this project is trying to evaluate.

**There is no ticket text.** No `description`, no `short_description`. Every
paper in the accompanying literature review performs text classification, and
none of it is possible on this log. The pivot to the decision layer is forced by
the data, not chosen.

One more property matters and is reported rather than smoothed: **priority 2
breaches more often than priority 1** (97.11% against 80.54%), and priority 3 is
95% of the data. Any aggregate number here is effectively a priority-3 number.

### The split, and a base-rate shift inside it

Chronological 70/15/15 by `opened_at`, with the cut pushed past tied timestamps
so one moment never straddles two splits. Train ends 2016-05-02 15:15; test
starts 2016-05-16 14:31.

The breach rate is **44.2% in train against 24.4% in validation and test**. Three
diagnostics were run before any model work, because a shift like that is usually
a bug:

* *Right-censoring — ruled out.* `resolved_at` is missing for 0.0% of incidents
  in every week from November 2016 to early February 2017; only the log's final
  week (14 incidents) spikes to 71%.
* *Left-truncation — ruled out.* Exactly 0.0% of incidents read
  `made_sla == False` on their first record, so the log does not open with a
  breached backlog.
* *What it actually is.* Weekly breach rates run 57.6%, 96.1%, 67.6%, 44.2%,
  33.6% over the first five weeks and settle at 18–27% from mid-April. The
  training period is dominated by that opening regime.

A sensitivity excluding the log's first three weeks was **declared in writing
before the test period was touched**, so that dropping them later could not be a
reaction to a test number.

### 3A. What the running demo is seeded with

The demo database is separate from everything above, and it holds no result. It
matters only because it is what a reader sees on screen, so what is in it is
stated here rather than left to be inferred.

**The tickets are real incidents, from the held-out split.** Each of the
fourteen is a UCI-498 record carrying its own incident number, priority,
category, subcategory, location and contact type. All fourteen come from the
`test` split — the 3,499 incidents neither model saw while being fitted. That
restriction is the point of the paragraph: a demo seeded from training rows puts
the model's own memorised examples on screen and calls the output a prediction.
`tests/servicedesk/test_demo_seed_provenance.py` checks each seeded incident
against the dataset's `split` column and each field against the record, so this
is a failing test rather than a claim.

**The category is what routing reads, and it is a proxy.** UCI-498 has no
resolver-skill column, so a seeded ticket's `required_skill` is the category the
incident was actually filed under, and the seeded roster holds skills with those
same names. That is a proxy and should be read as one: a real deployment maps
categories to the skills its resolvers actually hold. Naming the categories
something more readable — `Network` for `Category 42` — would have hidden the
proxy behind an invented field, which is the one field dispatch routes on.

**The incidents are chosen, and the choice is in the repository.** 3,333 of the
3,499 held-out records are priority 3, so a random draw would show one tier and
one dispatch outcome. `scripts/select_demo_incidents.py` picks a mix that
reaches all three: ten Category 42 tickets against nine Category 42 slots force
a queued decision, one Category 23 ticket forces the refusal because no seeded
resolver holds it, and two are placed far enough back to be overdue on arrival.
A live batch returns 11 recommended, 2 queued, 1 no eligible agent.

**One field is the demo's own, and it is recorded as such.** These incidents
opened in 2016 and the application measures a deadline against the clock, so
each ticket is opened at a stated offset inside the demo window instead. The
offset is written into the ticket's `TICKET_CREATED` event alongside the
provenance string, so the audit trail carries the transformation rather than a
document beside it describing one.

**What is still illustrative, and says so.** The four resolvers, the SLA policy
rows and the machine register. UCI-498 has no roster, no SLA policy and no asset
table, so those are invented figures, each row carrying a required source note.
The interface states the whole of this in one line in the footer rather than as
an alert repeated on every screen: an alert bar is for a condition a reader has
to act on, and the one that still qualifies is "no risk artifact loaded,
dispatch blocked".

## 4. Models

Both are trained offline. The API only loads a frozen artifact and scores a
validated feature vector; nothing trains at request time. Each artifact ships
with a model card and a metrics file.

### 4.1 SLA-risk model — `uci-risk-2026-09-10-a1`

Regularised logistic regression over one-hot encoded structured fields available
at creation, with isotonic probability calibration fitted on the validation
period. Baseline is the training-only empirical `P(breach | priority)`.

Locked test period, scored once after the artifact was frozen. Test base rate
**24.4%**, n = 3,499.

| Model | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC | Brier | ECE | Mean predicted |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| baseline `P(breach \| priority)` | 0.5505 | 0.3020 | 0.2142 | 0.1974 | 0.442 |
| logistic, uncalibrated | 0.7392 | 0.4896 | 0.1904 | 0.1664 | 0.411 |
| **logistic + isotonic on validation (shipped)** | **0.7383** | **0.4707** | **0.1608** | **0.0406** | **0.280** |
| logistic + isotonic on train CV (sensitivity) | 0.7385 | 0.4900 | 0.1894 | 0.1692 | 0.414 |
| warm-up excluded (predeclared sensitivity) | 0.7578 | 0.4987 | 0.1555 | 0.0307 | — |

Three readings, and the first is uncomfortable for the baseline:

1. **Priority is a weak ranker inside the test period.** It separates 17.3% from
   97.1% breach across the whole log, but priority 3 is 95% of the data, so as a
   ranker the baseline scores ROC-AUC 0.5505 — barely better than a coin. The
   candidate's 0.7383 clears it, and PR-AUC 0.4707 against a 24.4% base rate is
   the number that matters for a review queue.
2. **Calibration is where the base-rate shift shows.** Uncalibrated, the model
   predicts a mean of 0.411 against an observed 0.244: it learned the training
   period's 44% world. Isotonic recalibration on the validation period pulls the
   mean to 0.280 and ECE from 0.1664 to 0.0406. Calibrating *inside* the
   training period instead fixes nothing (ECE 0.1692). Recalibrating on recent
   data is not a trick; it is what has to happen when the base rate moves.
3. **The predeclared warm-up exclusion helps and stays a sensitivity.** It is the
   best row on every metric. The primary result is still the full split, because
   a sensitivity that becomes the headline after the fact is the move this
   project's own rules forbid.

At a stated review capacity of the top 10% of tickets, the shipped model reaches
precision 0.566 / recall 0.232; at 20%, 0.490 / 0.401; at 30%, 0.448 / 0.550.
The baseline at the same capacities: 0.346 / 0.142, 0.294 / 0.241, 0.272 / 0.335.

### 4.2 Duration model — `uci-duration-2026-09-10-a1`

Ridge regression on `log1p(minutes)`, inverse-transformed with `max(0, expm1(·))`,
against a training-only priority × category median baseline. Locked test, minutes:

| Model | Median abs. error | Mean abs. error | p90 abs. error |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| baseline (priority × category median) | 1,581.5 | 8,198.0 | 17,840.4 |
| **ridge on log1p (shipped)** | **979.0** | **7,984.0** | **17,747.4** |

Median actual duration in the test period is 201 minutes. This is, plainly, a
"wrong by most of a day" estimator on a target spanning minutes to months. It is
used for ordering projected finishes, and the model card says that logged
elapsed time contains historical queue waiting.

### 4.3 One bug worth reporting, because it failed silently

The application stores priority as an integer 1–4; the model was fitted on
UCI's label form (`"2 - High"`). Scoring an API-shaped payload returned 0.5556
for *every* priority: `OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore")` answers an
unrecognised level with an all-zero block and no error, so the most predictive
field was silently gone and the probability still looked plausible.
`feature_spec.normalise_priority` now maps both forms and raises on anything
else, and five tests score request-shaped payloads. A model that fails loudly
would have been caught on the first request; this one had to be looked for.

## 5. The dispatch policy

`api/servicedesk/services/dispatch.py`. Everything above the solve is a pure function
of plain numbers — no session, no ORM row, no clock — so a small instance can be
worked out on paper and checked against the code.

**Urgency, per ticket per minute.** Within target, the breach rate weighted by
the calibrated risk and by how little slack is left; past target, a separate
recovery branch that grows with lateness so already-breached work cannot be
starved by a stream of fresher tickets:

```
within target:  breach_rate × risk × (target_minutes / remaining_minutes)
overdue:        recovery_rate × (1 + overdue_minutes / grace_minutes)
```

**Candidate cost, per (ticket, resolver-slot) pair.** Three terms, each a rate
per minute multiplied by a number of minutes, so all three are currency and
adding them means something:

```
candidate_cost = urgency_rate      × projected_wait_minutes
               + late_rate         × projected_lateness_minutes
               + holding_rate      × projected_service_minutes
```

Assignment is Hungarian per priority tier, over one column per free WIP slot,
padded with visible queue columns so no ticket is silently dropped. P1 consumes
its slots before P2–P4 see the matrix. Ties are broken by a deterministic
epsilon derived from sorted identifiers, eight orders of magnitude below the
declared material cost quantum.

**The defect this replaces.** The earlier build put the *ticket's* predicted
resolution time into every qualified agent's column. A constant row carries no
information, so the assignment step could not prefer one resolver over another
and the solve was decoration. The `/legacy/assignment` screen still shows that
shape, which makes it a useful thing to demonstrate rather than hide. Here the
cost depends on the pair: when that resolver can start, how fast they are on
that skill, how late that combination finishes.
`test_the_cost_row_is_not_constant_across_agents` fails if the old shape returns.

**Every policy input is a visible business number.** There are no free `w1`,
`w2`, `w3` weights anywhere. A manager edits the rates in the application, and
each edit warns that it reaches future recommendations only.

### A divergence the report must state

The replay's policy writes the within-target branch as `breach × risk /
remaining`, which is currency per minute *squared*; multiplied by minutes it
yields currency per minute, and that was being added to two currency terms. The
shipped product uses the dimensionless slack ratio `target / remaining` instead.
Ticket ordering inside a tier is identical either way — `target_minutes` is
constant within a tier — but the balance between the wait term and the other two
is not. **The report therefore cannot claim that the replay evaluated the
shipped cost function.** It evaluated the same policy family with the
un-normalised urgency term. Section 7 shows that both terms involved were
already reported as null results.

## 6. Auditability

The requirement was "an immutable audit trail". Documenting one is not the same
as having one, so it is enforced by the database:

* recommendations are written once and never updated — acceptance and override
  append events beside them, so a ticket page can show what was recommended
  next to what a human actually chose;
* `ticket_events` carries `BEFORE UPDATE OR DELETE` triggers in PostgreSQL and
  `RAISE(ABORT, …)` triggers in SQLite. `DELETE FROM ticket_events` through a
  plain `sqlite3` connection returns `ticket_events is append-only`. In
  PostgreSQL deployment the application role additionally holds only SELECT and
  INSERT on that table;
* every mutating endpoint requires a signed-in actor, and the actor id is on the
  event;
* the model version, policy version, candidate costs and tie-break policy are
  snapshotted into each decision, so a stored recommendation stays readable
  after an artifact is replaced;
* the reason string is generated once from the decision's own payload and stored.
  The client displays it verbatim and never regenerates it, so what a dispatcher
  read in the demo is what the audit trail holds a year later.

## 7. The evaluation, including what it failed to show

`docs/results/replay-2026-09-10-a1.md`, run id `replay-2026-09-10-a1`.

3,499 held-out incidents. Each policy receives identical arrivals, identical
service demands, identical skill requirements, identical SLA policy and the same
seed. The exogenous job list is built **before** any policy runs, and invariant
tests prove that model predictions cannot alter stored service demand, that
replacing the risk scores leaves FIFO unchanged, and that no policy can read
`made_sla`, `resolved_at`, `closed_at`, the actual resolver, or any future row.

The simulated outcome is computed only as
`simulated_finish_time > configured_sla_due_time`. It is never sampled from the
risk model: the model influences priority, not the generated outcome.

Emulated workforce, from training rows only: 104 resolvers, 2,381 concurrent
slots at multiplier 1.0, WIP limits from the p90 concurrently-open incidents per
resolver, speed multipliers from training residuals shrunk toward 1.0. SLA
targets are **declared** (4 h / 8 h / 24 h / 48 h), not derived — deriving them
from the same durations that generate the service demand would compare a number
against itself. Capacity multipliers `{0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 1.00}` were fixed in
code before the first run.

### Simulated SLA breach rate, whole test period

| Capacity | FIFO | Priority-only | Duration-first | SLA-risk-aware |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0.10 | 0.7965 | 0.7897 | **0.5384** | 0.7791 |
| 0.25 | 0.3944 | 0.3935 | 0.3873 | **0.3732** |
| 0.50 | 0.3715 | 0.3718 | 0.3721 | **0.3327** |
| 1.00 | 0.3590 | 0.3581 | 0.3590 | **0.3287** |

### Three findings, two of them against the proposed policy

**1. The gain is candidate selection, not risk ranking.** At multiplier 1.0 the
proposed policy is −0.0294 against FIFO over the dense window, bootstrap
interval [−0.0422, −0.0180]. Queue waits are zero at that capacity, so ordering
cannot be doing the work — and the `no_agent_choice` ablation, which keeps
tiering and urgency but takes the first eligible slot, lands at 0.3607, back at
FIFO. What moves the number is routing to a faster resolver.

**2. The risk score contributed nothing measurable in this replay.** Replacing
every calibrated probability with 1.0 gives *exactly* the same 0.3287. So does
removing the projected-lateness term. Both are reported as null results, and
they are the reason the contribution sentence ends with "reported with its null
results". The risk model keeps its place in the product as Risk Radar ordering
and as the stored explanation on a recommendation — review aids — not as a
throughput claim.

**3. Under severe scarcity the proposed policy loses to shortest-job-first.** At
multiplier 0.10, duration-first simulates 0.5384 against the proposed policy's
0.7791, a difference of −0.2791 versus FIFO over the dense window with interval
[−0.3992, −0.1638]. The proposed policy's own difference there is −0.0161 with an
interval including zero, so under that much overload it is not distinguishable
from FIFO. This is classic SJF behaviour under overload. Nothing was retuned
after seeing it.

P1 breaches at about 89.7% under every policy, because the declared 240-minute
P1 target sits far below typical handling time in this log. That is a property
of the declared table meeting this data, not a policy result.

The bootstrap resamples arrival days inside the dense window only. Over the whole
test period the median arrival day holds two tickets and the busiest holds 358,
so resampling all 132 days would measure which near-empty day was drawn rather
than the ticket mix.

### What this evaluation cannot say

It is a counterfactual simulation under declared assumptions. It cannot observe
what would have happened to a historical ticket under a different resolver. The
metric is the **simulated SLA breach rate** and is never "SLA breach reduction".
Establishing a live effect needs a shadow deployment or an A/B test, and this
project performs neither.

The service-demand proxy — held-out empirical `resolved_at − opened_at` —
contains whatever queueing the ticket historically suffered, so it overstates
pure handling time and biases against *every* scheduling policy including the
proposed one. That makes it the conservative choice rather than a flattering one.

Twelve further assumptions are registered in `docs/assumptions.md`, each with
what is assumed, why the data cannot supply it, and where to change it.

## 7A. Hardware replacement

The title's third term is a screen, not an appendix. What makes it defensible is
where its numbers come from.

**They come from an operator.** UCI-498 has no hardware telemetry and no
ticket-to-asset key, and no public dataset supplies that join. So the machine
register is a table a manager fills in from their own purchasing and maintenance
records — age, purchase cost, resale value by year, running cost by year — and
every row carries a required source note. Nothing on the screen is mined,
modelled or predicted, and the interface says so above the table rather than in
a footnote.

**The arithmetic is the syllabus method, shown in full.** For replacement at the
end of year *n*,

```
total(n)   = purchase - resale(n) + running cost accumulated to n
average(n) = total(n) / n
```

and the optimal year is the one that minimises `average(n)`. The screen prints
the whole year-by-year table with the minimum marked, so a reader can recompute
the recommendation with a calculator. The second method — the renewal recursion
`B_i = N·p_i + Σ B_j·p_(i−j)` and the group-replacement average cost — answers
whether a fleet of like items is cheaper swapped on a fixed interval than
replaced as units fail.

Both are computed by `api/engine/replacement.py`, the same module the oral
examination is prepared against. The application calls it rather than
reimplementing it, because a second definition of a number that is checked by
hand is a second number.

**What "the machine is getting slower" means here.** The question the project
is answering — which machines need attention because they are degrading — has no
telemetry behind it, so it is answered where it can honestly be answered: in the
running-cost series. A machine that costs more to keep running each year is a
machine that is slower, breaks more often and takes more hands, and a rising
running cost against a falling resale value is exactly the condition the
deteriorating-item method exists to price. WS-1004's entered series runs 4,000
in year 1 to 32,000 in year 5, and the advisor says so in the sentence beside
the verdict, from those figures and no others. No slowness score is invented,
no failure is predicted, and the incident log contributes nothing to it.

**Tickets connect to machines only when a person says so.**
`tickets.machine_id` is set through one endpoint, by a signed-in user, and the
history records who did it. The advisor counts those links — how many tickets
and how many still open — and shows the count as context beside the verdict.
The count is not an input to the arithmetic.

A third count used to sit beside those two: how many of the linked tickets were
filed under a hardware category. It was removed when the demo moved to real
records, because it matched the literal string `hardware` and UCI-498 anonymises
its categories. No category in the real data means hardware, so the figure could
only ever read zero, and a zero that is a property of the schema rather than of
the machine reads on screen as evidence of absence. That is the general
limitation stated once: this dataset cannot tell a hardware incident from any
other kind, which is the same reason the register is operator-maintained.

**What this feature explicitly does not do.** It does not change a
recommendation, a deadline, an assignment or a risk score. A machine 21 months
past its replacement age changes what a manager is told and nothing the dispatch
policy computes. `test_replacement_advice_does_not_touch_dispatch` recommends
the same ticket before and after linking it to exactly such a machine and
asserts the resolver, the risk and the stored reason are identical.

That is also the honest correction to the earlier design. The previous build
multiplied a whole cost-matrix row by an ageing factor and called it a feedback
loop; a row scaled by a constant keeps its ordering, so the loop moved total
cost and could not move the assignment. This build does not claim the loop, and
section 9 says so.

## 8. Performance

`docs/performance.md`, generated by an opt-in benchmark rather than written by
hand. The full rescore path over 500 open tickets and 80 resolvers — load, read
policy and availability, run inference, write transactionally — runs at a median
of **87.5 ms** and p95 **137.8 ms** on the development machine (Windows, Python
3.12, SQLite), over 5 timed runs after one warm-up. That is a regression
baseline for one machine, not a service-level claim.

A microbenchmark of the Hungarian call itself was deliberately not used as a
performance claim: the realistic cost is model loading, database reads and
writes, not the assignment primitive.

## 9. How the title is answered

The registered title is fixed:

> AI-Driven SLA Optimization: Predictive Ticket Allocation, Escalation and
> Hardware Replacement in IT Service Desks

**Predictive Ticket Allocation** — the calibrated risk model, the duration
model, the tiered cost model and the per-tier Hungarian assignment, delivered as
recommendation, acceptance and override with an immutable record of each.

**Escalation** — an `ESCALATION_RECOMMENDED` event when calibrated risk crosses
the priority's manager-editable threshold, and an
`ESCALATION_ACKNOWLEDGED` event when a human answers it. The system recommends
and records. It routes nothing to a vendor or to L2 automatically, and the
acknowledgement changes no status, no assignee and no deadline.

**Hardware Replacement** — the machine register and its replacement advice,
described in section 7A. `api/engine/replacement.py` supplies the arithmetic;
the application supplies the register, the linked-ticket evidence and the
screen. A dispatcher sees which machines are past the replacement age their own
cost figures imply, and the table that says why.

The honest qualification, stated here rather than left for a question: **the
old degradation loop could not change which resolver serves a ticket.** The
factor multiplied a whole cost-matrix row, and a row scaled by a constant keeps
the ordering it had. It moved total cost, not the assignment. The
candidate-specific cost model in section 5 is the fix for dispatch, and the
machine register in section 7A is the fix for hardware: it advises on
replacement from an operator's own economics instead of pretending to influence
routing.

So **no dispatch result in this report contains a hardware claim**, and no
hardware number is presented as a routing improvement. There is no Backblaze
ingestion and no failure prediction; the register is decision support over
figures the organisation supplies, and the report never calls it a feedback
loop.

## 10. Operations Research content, for the oral examination

The eight syllabus modules stay runnable and hand-verifiable in `api/engine/`,
each on its own page under `/methods`, with the module-to-unit table in
`legacy/or_demo/README.md`. The worked instances are not in a notebook: each one
lives in the docstring of the test that checks it, so the expected answer is
written out by hand before the code runs and a regression in the arithmetic
fails the suite rather than sitting unread in a saved cell.

The service desk takes exactly one number from the engine — the average annual
cost table behind the Hardware screen, computed by `api/engine/replacement.py`.
That is deliberate: reimplementing the deteriorating-item method inside the
application would create a second definition of a figure the examiner checks by
hand. Nothing else in the product reads the engine, and dispatch reads none of
it.

The technique that carries into the production build is **assignment**: the same
Hungarian method the syllabus covers, applied per priority tier to a cost matrix
whose entries are business currency rather than raw minutes.
`test_hungarian_beats_greedy_when_the_greedy_pick_is_locally_cheap` builds a 2×2
instance, worked by hand in its docstring, where taking the cheapest cell first
leaves the second ticket with a prohibited column — which is the reason the
solver earns its place over a greedy loop.
