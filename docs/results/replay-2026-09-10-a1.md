# Replay results — `replay-2026-09-10-a1`

A counterfactual simulation of four dispatch policies over the locked test period, under declared capacity, skill and SLA assumptions. It compares policy behaviour in a simulator. It is not an A/B trial, and nothing here may be reported as an achieved reduction in SLA breaches.

## What was run

- jobs: 3,499 held-out incidents, fingerprint `43422ba67f59465a…`
- risk model `uci-risk-2026-09-10-a1`, duration model `uci-duration-2026-09-10-a1`
- service demand: held-out empirical duration (resolved_at - opened_at)
- breach rule: `simulated_finish_minute > configured_sla_due_minute`
- capacity multipliers: [0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0]
- workforce: 104 agents, 2,381 concurrent slots at multiplier 1.0, WIP from p90 concurrently open incidents per resolver, training period
- SLA policy: declared, not derived — illustrative demo policy; replace with organisational SLA policy. Not derived from UCI-498, which carries no SLA target.
- seed: 20260910

## Difference from FIFO, paired block bootstrap

Blocks are the arrival days inside the dense window; 1,000 draws; the same resample of days scores every policy. The sparse tail is excluded because a day holding two tickets is not a block — over the whole test period the median arrival day holds two tickets and the busiest holds 358, so resampling all 132 days would measure which empty day was drawn. The point estimate here is therefore the dense-window rate and differs slightly from the whole-period rate above. The interval describes variability in the ticket mix, not uncertainty about the queue dynamics, which are fixed once the fixtures are.

## What the results say

1. **At multiplier 1.0 the proposed policy simulates 0.3287 against FIFO's 0.3590** — a difference of -0.0294 over the dense window, bootstrap interval [-0.0422, -0.0180]. Queue waits are zero at this capacity, so the difference is not about ordering at all: it is about which agent the ticket goes to.

2. **Remove candidate selection and the difference disappears.** The `ablation_no_agent_choice` run, which keeps the tiering and the urgency ranking but takes the first eligible slot, simulates 0.3607 — indistinguishable from FIFO. The gain comes from the training-derived speed multiplier, not from the risk score.

3. **The risk score contributes nothing measurable here.** Replacing every calibrated probability with 1.0 (`ablation_no_risk`) gives 0.3287 against the full policy's 0.3287. Removing the projected-lateness term gives 0.3287; where that equals the full policy exactly, the term never changed a selection, because the slot ranking was already decided by projected service time.

4. **Under severe scarcity the proposed policy loses.** At multiplier 0.1, shortest-predicted-duration-first simulates 0.5384 against the proposed policy's 0.7791 — -0.2791 versus FIFO over the dense window, interval [-0.3992, -0.1638]. That is the classic shortest-job-first result: when the queue is deeply overloaded, clearing short work first finishes more tickets inside their windows. This is reported as it stands; no weight was retuned after seeing it.

   At that same capacity the proposed policy's own difference is -0.0161, interval [-0.0439, +0.0019] — an interval that includes zero, so under this much overload it is not distinguishable from FIFO either.

5. **P1 breaches at about 89.7% under every policy.** The declared P1 target is 240 minutes and typical handling time in this log is far longer, so no dispatch order can rescue it. That is a property of the declared SLA table meeting this data, and it is the clearest illustration of why the table is documented as an assumption rather than presented as a finding.

Taken together: this replay supports a narrow claim. Under these assumptions, choosing *who* gets a ticket using training-period productivity moves the simulated breach rate; ranking by calibrated risk did not, and under heavy overload a simpler shortest-job rule was better. The risk model still earns its place in the product as the Risk Radar's ordering and as the stored explanation on a recommendation, which are review aids, not throughput claims.

## Assumptions that decide these numbers

- shift coverage: agents are available for the whole simulated period
- initial queue state: the system starts empty
- preemption: none; a started job runs to completion
- WIP definition: concurrently open incidents, not concurrent effort
- handling-time proxy: logged elapsed time, which contains waiting
- SLA policy: declared, not derived from the log
- skill map: 26 categories mapped from training rows, modal-group purity median 0.70 and p25 0.38. Eligibility is therefore approximate, and that weakens every policy equally.
- agent speed: training-period residuals, shrunk toward 1.0 with k=20 and clipped to [0.5, 2.0]

## Limitations

- The service-demand proxy is logged elapsed time, which already contains historical queue waiting. It therefore overstates handling time, and at multiplier 1.0 it reproduces roughly the system that generated it.
- The simulation starts with an empty queue and agents always available. Neither is true of a real desk.
- The SLA targets are declared business inputs. A different target table moves every breach rate on this page.
- A policy's advantage here is an advantage in this simulator under these assumptions. Establishing a real effect needs a shadow deployment or an A/B test, neither of which this project ran.
